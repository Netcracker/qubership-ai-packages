# Pass 1 Discovery Brief: What Each Reader of a Change Description Needs, and Which Published Guidance Supplies It

## 1. Executive summary

**No single source covers all five readers; synthesis is required.** The published corpus is organized around who wrote it, not around who reads the output. Project guides written for email-patch or Gerrit workflows (Linux kernel, Git, PostgreSQL, OpenStack, LLVM) serve the archaeologist (R2) and reviewing maintainer (R1) very well and, through their stable/backport rules, the release manager (R5). Changelog and release-note specifications (Keep a Changelog, Common Changelog, towncrier) serve the upgrading user (R4). The on-call/support engineer (R3) is the worst-served reader: only the CVE "Key Details Phrasing" guidance and, indirectly, the kernel's "describe user-visible impact / include dmesg excerpts" rule speak to naming a symptom, a trigger, and affected versions in a way an operator holding a stack trace can grep. The best-served reader is R2 (the archaeologist): the "why, not how" tradition (Beams, Pope, Hutterer, kernel, Git, Google) is explicitly built for the person running `git blame` years later, and this is the one claim in the baseline with strong empirical backing — Tian et al. (ICSE 2022) found that across almost 1,600 messages from five highly active open-source Java projects, "an average of circa 44% of messages could be improved" because they "lack 'why' or 'what' information," and cite prior work finding "ca. 14% of commit messages in over 23,000 OSS projects were completely empty, 66% of the messages contained only a few words, and only 10% of commits had messages containing 'normal' descriptive English sentences."

**Is there an existing agent skill for description *structure* (not prefix/wording)?** The category is thin and immature. What exists is overwhelmingly (a) Conventional Commits prefix enforcement (commitlint, the many `/commit` skills) and (b) simple PR-description scaffolds (`## Summary / ## Changes / ## Testing / ## Notes`). These are template-shaped, not reader-derived: none attributes a slot to a named downstream reader, none has a rule for R3 or R5, and none is backed by evidence. The strongest structural agent artifact found is a "Git Commit Trailers" skill built around `git interpret-trailers`, but it governs footer metadata, not body slots. Conclusion: there is a genuine gap the proposed skill would fill.

**Which of the ten questions look answerable, and which are under-served:**
- Well-answerable now: Q1 (slots), Q3 (trailers), Q4 (versions/`Fixes:`), Q5 (squash-merge), Q7 (backport signals), Q9 (evidence).
- Partially answerable: Q2 (greppability — asserted widely, measured rarely), Q6 (changelog as on-call artifact — the framing exists but is scattered), Q8 (anti-patterns — named but rarely reader-attributed).
- Under-served: Q10 (agent skills for structure), and the specific empirical question of *what readers read first* (only Tao et al. 2012 comes close, and it is single-company).

**Where Pass 2 should concentrate:** a combination, weighted toward (1) the research literature (to anchor the slot ordering and greppability claims in measurement rather than repetition) and (2) tooling behavior (because tools like release-please, towncrier, and GitHub squash settings mechanically force what the author must write and therefore constrain the skill). Project guides are largely a solved discovery problem; the marginal return is in reconciling their contradictions, not finding more of them.

Where sources are silent (R3 read-first, R4 read-first ordering, R5 reading behavior), the entries marked "asserted" are my inference from the artifact each reader can actually reach at the moment of need: an on-call engineer has a log line and a version, not a diff, so the changelog is the only artifact indexed by their search terms. That inference is reinforced by Tao et al.'s finding that when a change description is *absent* or uninformative, rationale becomes hard to acquire — the artifact the reader opens matters only if it carries the identifiers they search on.

## 4. Promising shortlist for Pass 2

1. **Linux kernel "Describe your changes" (#1) + stable-kernel-rules (#2).** The kernel is the only body of guidance that explicitly serves four of five readers in one place: it orders the body as problem → user-visible impact → solution, tells authors to include dmesg excerpts and crash descriptions (R3), and uses `Fixes:`/`Cc: stable` to route the change to the backporter (R5). Workflow assumed: email patches — the risk is that patch-series and `---`-separator conventions do not transfer to squash-merge repos. But the *content* rules (impact, provoking circumstances, affected versions) transfer cleanly and are exactly what the baseline lacks for R3 and R5.

2. **Google eng-practices, CL descriptions (#6).** Monorepo, review-first workflow that closely matches modern GitHub practice. Contributes the first-line-stands-alone rule and body = problem + why-this-approach + known shortcomings. Uniquely, it states that tools generating CLs "should be configured to produce descriptions that follow these same guidelines" — direct license for the agent skill. Serves R1 and R2; transfers well because it is workflow-light.

3. **Tian et al. ICSE 2022 (#20) + Tao et al. FSE 2012 (#21).** These two anchor the whole skill in measurement rather than repetition. Tao ranks reader needs — rationale (I-1) is the single most important information need, while assessing a change's risk (I-9) and consistency (I-10, "are there other places that need similar changes") are the hardest to acquire — and reports that "more than 36%" of engineers understand code changes several times a day, with reviewing others' changes the top scenario (67.2%). Tian shows ~44% of real messages fail the Why/What bar and gives a coded taxonomy of *how* good messages express Why (describe error scenario ≈20%, illustrate requirement, imply necessity) and What (summarize object of change ≈57%). Together they justify the baseline's "why for `git blame`" claim and supply the slot vocabulary. Caveat: Tian is five OSS Java projects; Tao is single-company (Microsoft) — generalizability is limited.

4. **Li & Ahmed ICSE 2023 (#22).** Promising precisely because it *contradicts* a common shortcut. Its abstract states that although issue/PR links "referenced in a commit message [have] been treated as a way of providing Why information," the authors "found several quality issues that could hamper the links' ability to provide Why information." This decides a contradiction (see gaps) in favor of writing the why into the message, not delegating it to a tracker — directly relevant to R2 and R3.

5. **Keep a Changelog (#11) + Common Changelog (#12).** The R4 backbone and the source of the six-group structure in the baseline. Common Changelog adds sharper, more transferable structural rules (one line per entry, imperative mood, breaking-changes-first, long detail lives in the commit not the changelog) and the principle that a clean changelog "starts with a clean git history" — useful for a repo-independent skill.

6. **CVE "Key Details Phrasing" (#13).** The single best answer to the under-served R3 reader. Its MITRE template — `[VULNTYPE] in [COMPONENT] in [VENDOR][PRODUCT][VERSION] allows [ATTACKER] to [IMPACT] via [VECTOR]` — is a ready model for a `Security`/`Fixed` entry that names the symptom, the trigger, and the affected versions an operator will grep. It also warns that under-reporting key details makes later matching impossible while over-reporting obscures the distinguishing detail — a directly transferable balance rule.

7. **Kubernetes release-notes guide + release-note block (#16).** The best worked example of the squash-merge case the baseline is weak on: reviewer-facing content (test plan, `/kind`, checklist) stays in the PR, while a fenced ```release-note``` block carries the user-facing sentence into the generated changelog, and an empty block triggers a `do-not-merge/release-note-label-needed` label. Workflow: GitHub squash + Prow — the label mechanism is Kubernetes-specific, but the separation principle transfers.

8. **towncrier (#18) + release-please (#19).** These bracket the tooling design space: towncrier forces a hand-written, user-facing per-PR fragment ("news convenient to those that hear it, not those that write it" — the author decides what R4 sees); release-please derives the changelog mechanically from Conventional Commit types (the prefix decides). Studying both tells Pass 2 what each *forces the author to write* and, crucially, what each *loses* — neither captures a greppable symptom for R3.

9. **git interpret-trailers (#5) + GitHub closing keywords (#15).** Together they define the machine-readable reference layer: what a trailer block legally looks like (key/value pairs, dashes replacing spaces in tokens, grouped without blank lines at the end), and which trailers (`Closes #`, `Co-authored-by`) platforms actually parse and act on. Essential for the trailers question (Q3) and fully transferable.

## 5. Obvious rejects

- **Angular CONTRIBUTING prefix-vocabulary debates / commitlint type lists** — prefix enforcement with no reader in view; the baseline already fixes a prefix, and the type taxonomy serves release tooling, not R3.
- **Listicles restating Chris Beams** (codefinity, "7 best practices" blogs, most Medium posts) — no source, no example, wording-focused; reject except as evidence of what is widely repeated.
- **Changelog SaaS marketing pages** (Featurebase, Amoeboids, Changelogdev, and similar) — product marketing; the occasional good rule is borrowed from Keep a Changelog, so cite the primary.
- **Commit-message *generation* model papers** (CoRaCommit, ChatGPT-CMG, the LLM-CMG surveys) — out of scope; they measure generation quality, not what a good message contains (Tian and Li & Ahmed are the kept exceptions because they measured content).
- **Tim Pope essay as a *structural* authority beyond the model message** — its lasting contribution is the 50/72 template, which is mostly wording (out of scope); keep only for the subject/body split.
- **Conventional Commits as an R3 source** — a `fix:` prefix helps a release tool and does nothing for an on-call engineer holding a stack trace; include it for R4/R5 only, and say so.
- **Generic "how to write a changelog" LinkedIn/blog advice** — unsourced, audience-agnostic, no reader attribution.

## 6. Gaps and questions for Pass 2

- **Contradiction: issue link vs. inline why.** Tian et al. treat an issue/PR link as a valid way to supply "Why"; Li & Ahmed find those links often fail to deliver it (stale, or the linked issue lacks rationale). The archaeologist (R2) and on-call (R3) needs decide this in favor of writing a one-line why *and* the identifiers into the message itself, because the tracker may be unreachable during an incident or years later. Pass 2 should adopt the inline rule and treat the link as supplementary.
- **Workflow false-positives.** Kernel patch-series conventions, the `---` separator (content deliberately excluded from the commit), and `Cc: stable` are email-workflow artifacts. In a GitHub squash repo the equivalent of the `---` cut is "reviewer content stays in the PR, not the squashed body" — the same principle, different mechanism. Pass 2 must translate, not transplant.
- **Changelog owner: R4 vs. R3.** Most changelog guidance implicitly serves the upgrading user (R4). Only Common Changelog and the CVE phrasing lean toward the on-call reader (R3) by demanding the symptom text. Pass 2 should decide whether a `Fixed` entry is written for R4 (what changed) or R3 (the symptom to grep); the evidence suggests it must serve both, with the symptom named first.
- **What readers read first — thin evidence.** Only Tao et al. (2012, Microsoft) measures information needs, and it does not rank commit-vs-issue-vs-diff as "opened first"; it establishes only that rationale is primarily obtained from the check-in/commit message. No study observes on-call engineers or backporters reading descriptions. This is the biggest empirical hole; Pass 2 should flag conclusions here as inference.
- **Squash-merge published guidance is genuinely sparse.** GitHub documents the *behavior* (PR title + body seeds the squash commit, with the PR number appended) and Kubernetes documents a *label-driven* separation, but almost no project publishes explicit author guidance for "write your PR title as the future commit subject." This is a real gap the skill can fill with a defensible rule rather than a cited one.
- **What only a human can judge:** whether a rejected alternative is worth recording; whether a change is "serious enough" to backport (the kernel's own "oh, that's not good" test is explicitly human); the severity of a symptom; and whether an absent section is genuine non-applicability or an omission.

### Note on freshness and source quality
Empirical claims are drawn from peer-reviewed venues (FSE 2012, MSR 2015, EMSE 2016, TSE 2020, ICSE 2022, ICSE 2023) and quoted from the official PDFs or publisher pages. Platform behavior (GitHub squash defaults, Prow labels) reflects current 2023–2026 documentation. Essays (Pope 2008, Hutterer 2009, Beams 2014) are retained despite age because contribution guides still link them — but their durable contribution is wording, most of which is out of scope for this structural skill. Where a rule is widely repeated but never measured (greppability, "read breaking changes first"), the report labels it asserted rather than studied.
